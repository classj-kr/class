[E3-01] (고대 이집트의 그림 문자)
Ancient Egyptian hieroglyphs could be written in rows or in columns. The signs were placed in neat square groups instead of being lined up one by one. A row could run from left to right or from right to left, so readers needed a clue about where to begin. The clue was in the signs themselves. Signs shaped like people and animals in a line all faced the same way. A reader started from the side that those faces were looking toward. On the two sides of a doorway, the signs often faced opposite ways, so the two sides looked like mirror images of each other.

In ancient Mesopotamia, people wrote on small tablets of wet clay and let them dry. Many of these tablets were not stories but everyday records, such as loans and sales. A dried tablet was hard, but someone could still wet it and change a number. To stop this, writers sometimes wrapped a finished contract in a thin layer of clay, like an envelope. They wrote the same words, or a short version of them, on the outside and pressed their seals into it. If people later argued about the deal, a judge could break the envelope. Then he compared the outside with the copy that had stayed safe inside.

[E3-03] (잉카의 매듭 줄)
The Inca Empire kept its records on knotted strings called quipus. A quipu had one thick main cord, and many thinner cords hung down from it. Knots on these cords stood for numbers. The kind of knot and its place on the cord showed its value. Knots for ones were tied farthest from the main cord, while knots for tens and hundreds were tied higher up. When a place had no knot at all, it meant zero. Some quipus also had a cord that recorded the total of a group of other cords. With these records, officials could keep track of people, crops, and animals across the empire.

[E3-16] (돌이 된 나무)
Petrified wood is a fossil in which a tree has slowly turned into stone. It begins when a fallen tree is quickly buried under mud, sand, or volcanic ash. Being buried keeps out the air, so the wood rots very slowly. Groundwater carrying dissolved minerals then soaks into the wood. Over a long time, the minerals fill the tiny spaces in the wood and take the place of the wood itself. Because this happens little by little, the stone can keep the shape of the tree rings. Small amounts of other minerals, such as iron, can give the stone bright red and yellow colors.

A coloured print from old Japan was not the work of one person. A publisher decided what would sell and paid everyone. An artist drew the design in ink. [E3-48] (깊은 바다에 가라앉은 것)
When a great whale dies, its body may sink to the deep sea floor. Hagfish and deep-sea sharks find it within hours, and they keep at it until the flesh is gone, which can take a year or two. Bare bones are left lying in the mud. Soon the bones are covered with worms, and later with mats of bacteria and beds of shellfish. Robot submarines have found more than a hundred kinds of animal around a single skeleton, and some of them have never been seen anywhere else. A place like this can stay crowded for decades.
